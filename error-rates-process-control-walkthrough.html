error-rates-process-control.R: false positives and false negatives as process-control error rates

Walkthrough: how the script works, and how to use it in Quarto

Aims

error-rates-process-control.R reframes false-positive and false-negative rates — concepts usually introduced as abstract properties of a hypothesis test — as properties of a decision procedure applied over and over, in the sense Neyman and Pearson built their theory of hypothesis testing around (Neyman and Pearson 1933). It does this in two steps, deliberately concrete before abstract:

  • Part A starts from something that needs no statistical machinery at all: ART3, the author-recognition test from psych-battery-dgp.R’s battery (Vermeiren et al. 2023), already produces real false positives (saying “yes” to a name that isn’t an author) and real false negatives (saying “no” to a name that is) for every simulated participant. This script turns that into a decision rule — “classify someone as a frequent reader if they recognize at least k of the 60 authors” — and asks the question a rule like that actually needs answering: not “was this one classification right?”, but “if this exact rule were applied over and over, how often would it be wrong, and in which direction?”
  • Part B points out that a significance test at a fixed alpha is exactly this kind of rule — “reject H0 if p < alpha” — and reuses psych-battery-dgp.R’s sweep engine to show power, the empirical Type I rate, sign consistency (Type S), and CI coverage behaving exactly like ART3’s rule’s error rates did: stable long-run properties of a fixed procedure, recoverable by repetition, not properties of any one study’s result. A fourth property gets its own figure: the Type M “exaggeration ratio” (Gelman and Carlin 2014) — not whether the procedure’s decision is right, but how distorted the number it reports tends to be, conditional on having rejected.

Perezgonzalez (2015) is a useful accessible companion here: it lays out, side by side, how Fisher’s evidential reading of a single p-value differs from Neyman and Pearson’s decision-procedure reading — and how the hybrid most textbooks actually teach quietly borrows language from both without always saying so.

After working through it, you should be able to:

  • explain, using ART3’s own classification rule as a worked example, why a false-positive/false-negative rate is a property of a rule, estimable only by repetition, not a property of any single person’s test result;
  • generate a reliability-by-sample-size or a true-correlation-by-sample-size error-rate heatmap for a battery pair of your choosing, and read off where a fixed testing procedure is well- vs. poorly-behaved;
  • explain why a significance filter inflates reported effect sizes at low power, and why that inflation is a property of selecting on significance, not a flaw in any individual study;
  • state, in your own words, the difference between Neyman and Pearson’s long-run error-rate framing of a significance test and Fisher’s evidential reading of a p-value.

  • Reader background: the measurement-error/sampling-error distinction from sampling-measurement-error-walkthrough.qmd and the NHST/power framing from nhst-power-walkthrough.qmd are both drawn on directly here and are worth reading first if unfamiliar; some exposure to the terms “sensitivity”/“specificity” or “true positive rate”/“false positive rate” helps for Part A but is defined as it comes up.

How it works: the process

The two halves share a structure even though they look different: both repeat a fixed procedure many times (Part A: classify n people, reps times, at each threshold k; Part B: run a significance test on n participants, reps times, at each grid cell) and summarize what fraction of those repetitions landed in each outcome category. Nothing in Part B is new simulation machinery — it is entirely orchestration over psych-battery-dgp.R’s existing run_sweep()/summarize_sweep(), the same engine nhst-power.R uses, now read through a different lens.

Element-by-element walkthrough

Part A: ART3 as a concrete anchor

simulate_art3_battery() and plot_art3_rates()

d <- simulate_battery(n, reliability = list(ART3 = rel_val), seed = seed, keep_latent = TRUE)
hits    <- round((1 - d$ART3_fn_rate) * sp$split[["authors"]])
rejects <- round((1 - d$ART3_fp_rate) * sp$split[["foils"]])
true_ability <- (d$ART3_theta - cal$theta_mu) / cal$theta_sigma

Rather than re-implementing ART3’s item-level draw, this reuses simulate_battery()’s existing ART3_fn_rate/ART3_fp_rate columns and backs out the raw hits/rejects counts from them — one less place for the two codebases to drift apart. true_ability re-derives the standardized ability simulate_battery() doesn’t return directly (only the logit-scale theta), by inverting the calibration it was built from.

plot_art3_rates(n = 500, seed = 1)
NoteWhy these two rates move together

cor(fp_rate, fn_rate) across a large simulated sample comes out strongly positive (around .8), and the figure above shows it: people with a low false-negative rate also tend to have a low false-positive rate. This is a direct consequence of a modeling choice documented in psych-battery-dgp.R — ART3 uses a single probability p per person for both correctly recognizing an author and correctly rejecting a foil (hits ~ Binomial(60, p), rejects ~ Binomial(30, p)), rather than the two separate parameters (sensitivity and response criterion) a full signal-detection-theory model would use. That’s a deliberate simplification for this teaching battery, not an attempt at a psychometrically faithful recognition-memory model — worth knowing before reading too much into the exact shape of this scatter, even though the false-positive/false-negative rates themselves are genuine.

evaluate_art3_rule(): one application of a decision rule

truth_positive     <- d$ART3_theta > cal$theta_mu   # true ability above the population average
predicted_positive <- hits >= k                      # the rule's call

tp <- sum(truth_positive & predicted_positive)
fn <- sum(truth_positive & !predicted_positive)
fp <- sum(!truth_positive & predicted_positive)
tn <- sum(!truth_positive & !predicted_positive)

The rule being evaluated — “classify as a frequent reader if hits >= k” — needs a ground truth to be right or wrong against, and theta_mu (the population’s average true recognition ability, on the calibrated logit scale) supplies one: being truly above average is independent of how the noisy classification turns out, which is exactly the property a ground truth needs.

evaluate_art3_rule(n = 300, k = 40, seed = 2)
    n  k reliability n_pos n_neg      tpr        fnr       fpr       tnr
1 300 40         0.9   164   136 0.902439 0.09756098 0.1323529 0.8676471

One call, one sample, one set of rates — and, by itself, not yet informative about the rule: a different seed at the same n and k will give a visibly different fpr/tpr pair. That instability is the reason Part A doesn’t stop here.

sweep_art3_rule() and plot_art3_roc(): the rule’s long-run operating curve

sweep_small <- sweep_art3_rule(n = 300, k_grid = c(30, 40, 50), reps = 50, seed_base = 3)
aggregate(cbind(fpr, tpr) ~ k, sweep_small, mean)
   k         fpr       tpr
1 30 0.622286589 0.9987836
2 40 0.094747331 0.8515409
3 50 0.000137931 0.2162233

sweep_art3_rule() repeats evaluate_art3_rule() at each threshold in k_grid, drawing a fresh sample of n people each time — the single mechanism that turns “one classification’s noisy outcome” into “the rule’s actual operating characteristics,” by averaging over many independent applications rather than trusting any one of them.

plot_art3_roc(n = 300, k_grid = seq(25, 50, by = 5), reps = 200, seed_base = 2)

Read the pale cloud and the red line as two different claims. The cloud — one dot per replicate — is how noisy a single application of the rule’s reported false-positive/true-positive rate can be, even at a respectable n = 300. The red line connects each threshold’s average over 200 replicates, and it traces a textbook ROC-style trade-off: tightening k (demanding more recognized authors before calling someone a frequent reader) buys a lower false-positive rate at the cost of a lower true-positive rate, and no threshold minimizes both. That trade-off — not any particular point on it — is the property of the rule, in the Neyman-Pearson sense: knowable in advance, by simulation or by theory, before the rule is ever applied to a real person.

Part B: the same logic, abstracted to a fixed-alpha NHST procedure

run_reliability_sweep() and plot_reliability_heatmap(): holding the right thing fixed

This is the script’s most distinctive piece of code, and it exists because of a real bug caught while building this script — worth reading in full:

run_reliability_sweep
function (var_x, var_y, n_grid, reliability_grid, true_corr_latent = NULL, 
    reps = 200, alpha = 0.05, seed_base = 1234) 
{
    if (is.null(true_corr_latent)) {
        rel_default <- default_reliability_vec()
        r_obs_default <- default_r_obs_matrix()[var_x, var_y]
        true_corr_latent <- disattenuate(r_obs_default, rel_default[[var_x]], 
            rel_default[[var_y]])
    }
    grid <- expand.grid(n = n_grid, reliability = reliability_grid, 
        rep = seq_len(reps))
    rows <- vector("list", nrow(grid))
    for (i in seq_len(nrow(grid))) {
        n <- grid$n[i]
        rel <- grid$reliability[i]
        rep_i <- grid$rep[i]
        seed_cell <- seed_base + rep_i * 1e+06 + n * 1000 + round(rel * 
            1000)
        implied_obs <- true_corr_latent * rel
        d <- simulate_pair(n, var_x, var_y, true_corr = true_corr_latent, 
            corr_scale = "latent", reliability_x = rel, reliability_y = rel, 
            seed = seed_cell)
        fit <- fit_focal_cor(d, var_x, var_y)
        diag <- diagnose_cor_result(fit, implied_obs, alpha)
        rows[[i]] <- cbind(data.frame(n = n, true_corr = implied_obs, 
            reliability = rel, rep = rep_i), diag)
    }
    out <- do.call(rbind, rows)
    attr(out, "true_corr_latent") <- true_corr_latent
    out
}
NoteA real bug this function fixes

The first version of plot_reliability_heatmap() called psych-battery-dgp.R’s run_sweep() directly, sweeping reliability_grid while holding true_corr fixed on the observed scale (run_sweep()’s default interpretation) — e.g. “hold StuVoc1-GK’s correlation at .67 while reliability drops from .9 to .3.” That request is not always satisfiable: disattenuating an observed r = .67 back through a shared reliability of .3 requires a latent correlation above 1, which disattenuate() can only handle by capping it at .999 and issuing a warning. Run across a whole reliability grid, that produced a wall of repeated capping warnings and, worse, a heatmap whose low-reliability cells were all silently simulating the same near-1.0 latent correlation rather than the requested .67 observed correlation — a wrong figure that happened not to crash.

The fix is the same device sampling-measurement-error.R’s simulate_attenuation_sweep() already uses: hold the latent (construct-level) correlation fixed instead, and let the observed correlation — and therefore its significance, sign, and coverage — respond to reliability, which is the actual point of this figure. true_corr_latent * reliability (both tests share one reliability value here) gives the implied observed correlation at each cell, which is what actually gets tested for significance/sign/coverage, and which is written into the output’s true_corr column so summarize_sweep() can be reused with no changes at all.

plot_reliability_heatmap("StuVoc1", "GK", n_grid = c(20, 50, 100, 200, 400),
                          reliability_grid = seq(0.3, 0.9, by = 0.1), reps = 150, seed_base = 3)

Power climbs with both axes, as it should: more participants and a more reliable measure both make a real effect easier to detect, and the figure makes visible just how much reliability alone costs — at reliability = .3, even n = 20 only reaches 19% power, where the same n at reliability = .9 already reaches 97%.

plot_true_corr_heatmap() and plot_process_control_overview()

plot_true_corr_heatmap("StuVoc1", "GK", n_grid = c(20, 50, 100, 200, 400),
                        true_corr_grid = c(0, 0.2, 0.67), reps = 200, seed_base = 4)

Unlike plot_reliability_heatmap(), this one can safely reuse run_sweep() directly, because reliability is held fixed at a sensible default rather than swept down toward the disattenuation ceiling — the true_corr = 0 row is the empirical false-positive rate, and it should (and does) stay low and flat across every n, unlike the other two rows.

All three panels answer the same underlying question Part A’s ROC figure did — “what happens if this exact procedure is applied over and over?” — just for a significance test rather than a classification rule. The true_corr = 0 curves are the figure’s real payoff: a flat, low power/Type I-rate line and a (largely undefined, since there’s no “correct sign” under a true null) blank sign-consistency panel are what a well-behaved testing procedure looks like when there’s nothing there to find, in exactly the sense Neyman and Pearson meant the “size” of a test’s rejection region to be fixed and known in advance (Neyman and Pearson 1933) — not inferred after the fact from how convincing any one result felt.

A fourth error rate, on its own axis: plot_type_m_curve()

summarize_sweep() computes one more quantity than the three panels above show: type_m_ratio, the median |estimate| / |true_corr| among significant results only — the Type M “exaggeration ratio” (Gelman and Carlin 2014). It answers a different question from power/Type I/sign-consistency/ coverage: not “does this procedure reach the right decision?”, but “when it rejects, how distorted is the number it hands back?”

NoteWhy this isn’t a fourth panel in plot_process_control_overview()

type_m_ratio is deliberately kept out of the three-panel overview above, not folded in as a fourth facet. Power, sign consistency, and coverage are all proportions bounded to [0, 1], which is what lets plot_metrics_vs_x() share one y-axis across them. An exaggeration ratio is 1 at no inflation and can comfortably exceed 2–3 at low power — sharing that [0, 1] axis would silently clip the part of the story that matters most. plot_type_m_curve() is a separate figure built around its own appropriately scaled axis instead.

plot_type_m_curve("StuVoc1", "GK", n_grid = c(10, 15, 20, 30, 50, 100, 200),
                   true_corr_grid = 0.2, reps = 500, seed_base = 6)

A true correlation of .2 (rather than this pair’s own ≈.67) is used here deliberately, for the same reason nhst-power-walkthrough.qmd sweeps a narrower n range for its corridor plot: a strong effect like StuVoc1–GK’s actual correlation reaches high power — and so, low exaggeration — almost immediately, leaving nothing dramatic to see. At a true correlation of .2 (the same weak-effect benchmark Vermeiren et al. (2023)’s own power illustration uses), a significant result at n = 10 overstates the true correlation by a median factor of roughly 3.5×; by n = 200 that’s fallen to close to 1×. Nothing is wrong with any individual significant study along the way — the inflation is a consequence of selecting on significance, which forces every significant result at small n to clear a high bar (|r| has to exceed roughly .44 to be significant at n = 20, for instance), and the smaller the true effect relative to that bar, the more a result has to be pulled upward by sampling variation to clear it at all.

Quarto usage / integration

This script is standalone, like the other three in this set — not sourced into doing-being-science.qmd. A typical setup chunk:

source("psych-battery-dgp.R")
source("error-rates-process-control.R")

Parameter quick reference

Function Parameter Default Meaning
evaluate_art3_rule() / sweep_art3_rule() / plot_art3_roc() k / k_grid — Classification threshold(s): “frequent reader” if hits >= k.
n — Participants per single application of the rule.
reps 200 Independent applications of the rule per threshold (sweep/plot only).
reliability NULL ART3’s reliability; NULL uses its Vermeiren-default value.
run_reliability_sweep() / plot_reliability_heatmap() reliability_grid — Reliability values to sweep (applied to both tests equally).
true_corr_latent NULL The true (disattenuated) correlation held fixed; NULL backs it out from the pair’s Vermeiren-default observed correlation.
plot_true_corr_heatmap() / plot_process_control_overview() true_corr_grid NULL (overview) / — (heatmap) Observed-scale correlations to sweep; the overview’s NULL default uses c(0, <pair's Vermeiren-default r>).
reliability NULL Reliability applied to both tests; NULL uses var_x’s Vermeiren-default value.
plot_reliability_heatmap() / plot_true_corr_heatmap() metric "power_or_type1" Which summarize_sweep() column to plot: "power_or_type1", "sign_consistency", or "coverage" (not "type_m_ratio" — see plot_type_m_curve()).

n_grid — Sample sizes to sweep.
(sweep-based functions) seed_base 1234 Base seed for deterministic per-cell seeding.

Common errors

Message Likely cause
`n` must be a positive integer n <= 0 passed to simulate_art3_battery()/evaluate_art3_rule().
`k` must be between 0 and 60 (the number of author items in ART3) k outside ART3’s valid range of recognizable authors.
`reps` must be at least 1 sweep_art3_rule() called with reps = 0.
`metric` must be one of power_or_type1, sign_consistency, coverage A misspelled or unsupported metric passed to either heatmap function.
Warning: achieved reliability (...) differs from the requested target (...) A reliability/reliability_grid value pushed calibration outside tolerance — see psych-battery-dgp.R’s calibrate_ability_general().
Warning: Target observed correlation r = ... is not achievable at ... plot_true_corr_heatmap()’s true_corr_grid includes a value too large for the reliability in effect; this is the exact warning run_reliability_sweep() was built to avoid when sweeping reliability itself (see the callout above).

Exercises

  1. Call evaluate_art3_rule(n = 300, k = 40, seed = s) for s in 1:10. How much does fpr vary across the ten single applications? Compare that spread to the tight red trajectory in plot_art3_roc()’s k = 40 point, built from 200 applications rather than one.
  2. Lower ART3’s reliability to 0.5 in plot_art3_roc() (via the reliability argument) and compare the resulting curve to the default (reliability = .9) version above. Does the trade-off get better or worse, and does that match what you’d predict from more measurement error?
  3. Run plot_reliability_heatmap() for StuVoc1-ART3 (a weaker pair, r ≈ .52) instead of StuVoc1-GK. At n = 100, how much more does power suffer from the same reliability drop, compared to the StuVoc1-GK heatmap above?
  4. Request plot_true_corr_heatmap("StuVoc1", "GK", n_grid = c(50, 200), true_corr_grid = c(0.67), reliability = 0.2) — deliberately low reliability with a high requested observed correlation. Confirm you get the “not achievable” warning from the Common Errors table, and explain in your own words why plot_reliability_heatmap()’s run_reliability_sweep() approach doesn’t have the same problem.
  5. Pick a metric other than the default ("sign_consistency" or "coverage") for plot_process_control_overview()’s underlying run_sweep() call (via summarize_sweep()’s output directly, since plot_process_control_overview() always shows all three). Which of the three looks most stable across n, and which looks most sensitive to it?
  6. Re-run plot_type_m_curve("StuVoc1", "StuVoc2", ...) (r ≈ .82, a much stronger effect than the true correlation of .2 used above) across the same n_grid = c(10, 15, 20, 30, 50, 100, 200). How much smaller is the exaggeration ratio at n = 10, and does it still fall toward 1 by n = 200, or has it already arrived there well before that?
